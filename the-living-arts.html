A letter for the artist who is doubting

This is not “just a hobby” anymore.

Dear artist, designer, musician, performer, writer and keeper of stories,

You chose a path without the neat certainty other people understand. That does not make your choice smaller. It makes your courage visible. You are deciding to study how humans have expressed love, resistance, faith, grief, identity and imagination across generations.

You are not beginning from nothing. Every brushstroke, rhythm, costume, building, poem, movement and story carries an echo of someone who worked before you. By learning older techniques and understanding their histories, you are not becoming trapped in the past. You are receiving something precious enough to transform.

Tradition survives when each generation loves it enough to learn it—and trusts itself enough to let it evolve.

Things will become difficult. Inspiration will disappear precisely when a deadline arrives. You will compare your beginning to somebody else's middle. You will create something terrible, then something almost right, then something unmistakably yours. You will doubt whether you belong.

Keep going. Greatness is not waiting on the comfortable side of the barrier. Skill grows through drafts, rehearsals, awkward attempts, patient observation and the courage to show your work again. The barrier is not evidence that you chose incorrectly. It is often evidence that you have reached the place where growth begins.

You made a beautiful choice.
— Jigyasa Verse

The past is not silent

You are carrying something forward.

Technology can reproduce an image in a second. It cannot replace the human memory inside a technique, the relationship between a teacher and apprentice, or the meaning a community gives to a form.

Study origins carefully. Name cultures specifically. Learn from community voices. Credit the people whose knowledge you enter. The goal is not to borrow a beautiful surface—it is to understand the living world beneath it.

A letter for art teachers and creative mentors

You keep the flame moving.

You teach what cannot be learned from instructions alone: how much pressure a material can hold, how to hear when a note settles, how to wait through a silence on stage and how to recognise when a work has finally said enough.

Your knowledge arrived through your own teachers, communities, ancestors, practice, failure and attention. Each time you demonstrate a technique, tell its history or encourage a frightened student to try again, you prevent that knowledge from disappearing.

You do not keep tradition alive by forcing it to remain unchanged. You give students roots deep enough to understand what they have inherited and wings brave enough to evolve it with care.

Thank you for passing on more than skill. Thank you for passing on memory, discipline, permission and possibility.

When doubt enters stage left

Give it one line.
Do not give it the whole play.

Make the smallest possible version of the thing you are afraid to begin.

The world has enough spectators.

Make something only you could make.
