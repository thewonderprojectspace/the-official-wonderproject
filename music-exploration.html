LISTENING BOOK · LEAF 01

There is no single first song

Music may be older than the stories we wrote down.

Sound leaves fragile evidence. A flute can survive in the ground; a melody carried only by voices may disappear from the archaeological record. Cultures across the world developed music in different ways, often combining rhythm, movement, poetry, ceremony and memory. This pathway offers selected windows—not one tidy origin story.

AN UNWRITTEN SCORE

Most early music left no recording.

Objects, images and written marks offer clues. Living oral traditions show how knowledge can travel without paper—but they should not be treated as unchanged fossils of the ancient past.

LISTENING BOOK · LEAF 02

When a poem needed breath

Lyrics were not decoration. They carried a way of seeing.

Many older texts that survive were preserved because communities considered them sacred, politically important or worth teaching. Their words often hold mortality, exile, devotion, injustice and the strangeness of being alive.

THE HYMN

Speaking toward mystery

Repetition made words memorable; melody made them communal. A hymn could hold awe and uncertainty in the same breath.

Try asking: what cannot be said plainly here?
THE LAMENT

Giving sorrow a form

Loss became pattern—returning phrases, descending contours, a voice answered by others. Suffering was not erased; it was witnessed.

Try asking: who is being asked to remember?
THE EPIC

A library carried by people

Sung and recited narratives could preserve ancestry, conflict, duty and warnings. Performance made memory a living responsibility.

Try asking: whose version becomes history?
THE MYSTIC SONG

Turning inward to travel beyond

Poets used the beloved, the road, thirst and light as images for spiritual longing. An intimate feeling opened into a larger question.

Try asking: is the “beloved” a person, the divine—or both?

A tempting claim, examined

Were old lyrics deeper because life held more suffering?

Sometimes suffering pushes a writer beneath the surface—but age alone does not make a song profound.

Older people were not permanently solemn, and today’s music is not only shallow. Ancient communities also sang for play, courtship, celebration and work. Modern artists still write about grief, faith, displacement, identity and injustice.

What changes our impression is survival: sacred hymns, royal texts and works copied by generations are more likely to reach us than yesterday’s forgettable tavern tune. Translation and distance can also make old language feel mysterious.

Depth begins when a song risks an honest question—not when it belongs to a particular century.

A VOW FOR THE CURIOUS LISTENER

पहले सुनो, फिर समझो।

First listen. Then try to understand.

I will not call unfamiliar sound primitive.

I will ask what a song does for its community.

I will respect living traditions, not freeze them in the past.

I will notice silence as carefully as sound.

I will let music change the question I arrived with.
