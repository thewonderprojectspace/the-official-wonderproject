The meeting begins

The desert looks empty until the room fills.

A mulgara arrives with the moisture in its prey. A Spinifex hopping mouse brings seeds and roots to the table. A beetle describes gathering fog. A thorny devil traces a path from its skin to its mouth. Nearby, a cactus waits for condensation that may or may not come.

They do not all live in the same desert. This is a meeting across drylands—a way to compare different strategies. Each one asks the same practical question: where can water enter, and where can it leave?

No one here is given an endless supply.
Every drop has a route.

How the desert saves

Attention is an adaptation

01 / Find

Count hidden sources

Food moisture, dew, fog and metabolic water may all contribute. Different species rely on different combinations.

02 / Avoid

Spend less in the heat

Nocturnal activity, burrows and torpor can reduce heat exposure and conserve water and energy.

03 / Retain

Limit what leaves

Efficient kidneys and concentrated urine help some mammals reduce water loss. Every adaptation has limits and trade-offs.

“Nothing gets wasted” is the meeting’s shorthand—not a claim that organisms lose no water. Urine, faeces and evaporation are real outflows. The intelligence is in meeting needs while reducing avoidable loss and making use of scarce sources.

The ledger closes

Survival is not abundance.
It is attention.

The desert does not promise a full cup. It offers brief rain, a trace of moisture, a meal, a cool burrow and the right moment to move. Life persists by noticing what is available—and by making each exchange count.
