Evidence room 01 · Meet the suspect

First correction: bacteria are not waiting to attack us.

Bacteria are ancient, diverse and everywhere. Many live harmlessly around us; many support life. A smaller number can cause infection—and some of those bacteria possess ways to survive antibiotic treatment.

An antibiotic is a medicine designed to kill bacteria or stop them from multiplying. Antibiotics transformed modern medicine. They make bacterial infections treatable and help make surgery, organ transplantation, intensive care and cancer treatment safer.

But antibiotics are not universal weapons. They do not work against viruses such as those responsible for colds and influenza. Using an antibiotic when it cannot help still exposes bacteria to the drug and can add unnecessary selection pressure.

Case correction
Statement corrected

“My body became resistant to antibiotics” is inaccurate. The person does not become resistant. The bacteria change, acquire resistance or are selected because they already possess it.

Evidence room 02 · The medicine enters

Four ways to interrupt a bacterial cell.

Different antibiotics act on different bacterial processes. The comparisons below are metaphors—not literal buildings—but they make the molecular investigation easier to see.

▦

Collapse the wall

Some antibiotics interfere with construction of the bacterial cell wall. Without a stable boundary, susceptible bacteria can rupture.

⚙

Stop the factory

Some bind to bacterial ribosomes and interrupt protein production—the cellular machinery needed for growth and repair.

⌁

Interrupt copying

Some interfere with processes needed to copy or manage bacterial DNA, preventing successful reproduction.

◇

Cut a pathway

Some block chemical pathways bacteria need to build essential molecules and continue growing.

Evidence room 03 · Natural selection

Watch the survivors change the population.

A bacterial population is not always genetically identical. By chance, a few organisms may already carry a mutation or resistance gene that helps them survive.

When an antibiotic is introduced, susceptible bacteria are removed. Resistant bacteria face less competition, reproduce and can become a much larger proportion of the next population.

BEFORE TREATMENT: most bacteria are susceptible. A small resistant minority is already present.
The antibiotic did not teach the bacteria how to resist. It changed which bacteria remained to reproduce.

Evidence room 04 · Resistance toolkit

Five escape routes hidden inside the cell.

Resistance is not one trick. Different bacteria can evade different antibiotics through distinct biological mechanisms.

The blocked door

The bacterium changes its outer structures or reduces entry, so less antibiotic reaches its target.

⌂

The changed lock

The antibiotic target changes shape. The medicine can no longer bind effectively to the place it once recognised.

⌁

The efflux pump

Transport proteins actively push the antibiotic back out before it can accumulate to an effective level.

→

The destroyer

The bacterium produces an enzyme that breaks down or chemically modifies the antibiotic.

×

The protected community

Some bacteria grow in biofilms— structured communities that can reduce penetration and contain slower-growing cells that are harder to eliminate.

◉

Evidence room 05 · The secret exchange

A bacterium does not always inherit resistance from its parent.

Resistance genes can also move horizontally between bacteria, sometimes even between different bacterial species.

Small circles of DNA called plasmids can carry resistance genes and move from one bacterial cell to another. Bacteria may also collect DNA from their surroundings, while viruses that infect bacteria can occasionally move genetic material between cells.

This is not conscious communication. The bacteria are not planning an escape. These are biological processes—but their effect can resemble information moving rapidly through a hidden network.

Evidence room 06 · Cause map

There was no single culprit.

Resistance is a natural evolutionary possibility. Human systems determine how often bacteria encounter antibiotics, how strongly they are selected and how easily resistant strains spread.

FILE 01

Unnecessary use

Antibiotics used when no bacterial infection is present create risk without providing the intended benefit.

FILE 02

Incorrect prescribing

The wrong drug, dose or treatment duration can expose bacteria without controlling the infection effectively.

FILE 03

Spread between people

Poor infection prevention allows resistant bacteria to move through hospitals, households and communities.

FILE 04

Animal and environmental routes

Antibiotic use and resistant organisms connect human health, animal health, food systems, soil and water.

FILE 05

Unequal access

Limited diagnostics, sanitation and reliable treatment can encourage both untreated infection and poorly targeted antibiotic use.

FILE 06

A thin development pipeline

Antibiotic discovery and development have struggled to keep pace with the emergence of resistant bacteria.

Interrogation room · Myth or evidence?

Four statements are waiting for questioning.

“My body has become resistant to the medicine.”

VERDICT: INCORRECT. Resistance occurs in the bacteria or other microorganisms—not in the human body.

“Antibiotics will help my cold or influenza clear faster.”

VERDICT: INCORRECT. Colds and influenza are caused by viruses. Antibiotics target bacteria, not viruses.

“A stronger antibiotic will always solve the problem.”

VERDICT: INCORRECT. Some bacteria resist multiple antibiotic classes. Broader treatment may also disturb helpful bacteria and add selection pressure, so therapy must be professionally targeted.

“Resistance belongs only inside hospitals.”

VERDICT: INCORRECT. Resistant bacteria and resistance genes can circulate through healthcare facilities, communities, animals, food and the environment.

Emergency room · The scale of the case

When treatment options disappear, modern medicine becomes more fragile.

A major 2024 global analysis estimated past and future deaths involving bacterial antimicrobial resistance. “Attributable” means the death would have been avoided if the resistant infection had instead been drug-susceptible.

1.14m Estimated deaths directly attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance in 2021.
1.91m Projected annual deaths attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance in 2050 under the reference scenario.
39.1m Projected cumulative deaths attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance from 2025 to 2050.

These are modelled estimates with uncertainty ranges, not a countdown or a future that cannot be changed. Source: GBD 2021 Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, The Lancet, 2024.

A common infection

may require longer, more complex or more toxic treatment.

A surgical procedure

becomes riskier if postoperative bacterial infections are difficult to treat.

Cancer treatment

depends on effective infection control while the immune system may be weakened.

A child with a serious infection

needs the right antibiotic, dose and duration—not simply the broadest medicine available.

Command centre · The response

The case is serious. It is not hopeless.

The goal is not to fear antibiotics or avoid them when they are needed. The goal is to preserve their power through accurate diagnosis, careful use, infection prevention, surveillance and research.

For individuals

  • Use antibiotics only when prescribed for you.
  • Do not use leftover or shared antibiotics.
  • Follow professional instructions for dose and duration.
  • Do not demand antibiotics for viral illnesses.
  • Use hygiene and vaccination to prevent infections.

For healthcare systems

  • Use diagnostic evidence where possible.
  • Choose the appropriate drug, dose, route and duration.
  • Review treatment when laboratory results arrive.
  • Track resistance and prescribing patterns.
  • Prevent transmission in every care setting.

For the wider world

  • Strengthen clean water and sanitation.
  • Improve access to vaccines and reliable diagnostics.
  • Use antibiotics responsibly across human and animal health.
  • Limit environmental contamination.
  • Invest in antibiotics and alternative strategies.
We cannot order evolution to stop. We can stop giving it unnecessary opportunities.
