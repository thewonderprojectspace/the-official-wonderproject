The Truth of Survival · Field Note 05

Born to Burn: When destruction becomes renewal

A forest does not always survive by avoiding fire. Sometimes it survives by remembering exactly what to do when the flames arrive.

8 minute read Fire ecology Scroll to ignite
Enter the burn

The first thing fire takes is the illusion of stillness. Bark cracks. Leaves lift into sparks. Air becomes weather of its own. To a human watching from the edge, the forest appears to be ending. But beneath the noise, thousands of older instructions are already being followed.

01 — A force, not a villain

The forest has met fire before.

Fire is not one single event. Ecologists speak of a fire regime: the characteristic frequency, intensity, season and pattern of burning in a place. Those details matter. A cool, patchy ground fire and an immense canopy-consuming blaze may carry the same name, yet leave radically different futures behind.

Across many grasslands, savannas, heathlands and dry forests, fire has helped shape which species live where. It opens dense vegetation, releases nutrients held in dead material and creates a shifting mosaic of shelter, sunlight and food. Some organisms tolerate it. Some evade it. Others have built fire into the timing of reproduction itself.

Fire does not merely act upon an ecosystem. Over evolutionary time, it can become one of the forces that helps compose it. Fire ecology: the study of relationships between fire, living systems and place

This does not make every fire “good.” It means the question is more precise than whether fire belongs. We must ask: which fire, how hot, how often, in what season—and in an ecosystem with what history?

02 — While the canopy burns

Survival moves out of sight.

As flames climb, life retreats into layers. Wombats and reptiles may use burrows. Insects shelter beneath bark or soil. Fungi persist through underground networks. Seeds wait in the earth or inside woody fruits. The visible forest is only one floor of a much deeper building.

Plants have their own architectures of endurance. Thick bark insulates living tissue. Protected buds wait beneath bark or inside swollen underground lignotubers. Deep roots store water and carbohydrates. When the crown is scorched, those reserves can push out new shoots along blackened trunks.

Resprout Protected buds and underground stores allow a plant to rebuild after leaves and branches are lost.
Release Heat dries or opens woody fruits, scattering seeds onto newly exposed ground.
Germinate Heat and smoke chemicals can interrupt dormancy and begin a seed’s next stage.

Fire can therefore remove one living form while creating conditions for another. Light reaches the ground. Competition changes. Minerals return to surface soil. Yet each advantage has limits: extreme heat can kill buried seeds, damage soils and consume refuges that animals need.

03 — When the rhythm breaks

Adapted to fire does not mean invincible.

A forest adapted to periodic burning may still collapse when fire returns too soon. Young plants can be killed before producing seed. Animals can lose the hollow trees and unburnt patches that once acted as refuges. Repeated severe fire may simplify a community rather than renew it.

Climate change is intensifying this problem in many regions by producing hotter, drier conditions and longer fire seasons. At the same time, historical fire suppression can allow fuels to accumulate in ecosystems that once experienced more frequent, lower-intensity burning. Land clearing, invasive species and fragmentation alter the result again.

Research after Australia’s 2019–20 bushfires illustrates why recovery cannot be assumed. UNSW researchers are monitoring fire-sensitive rainforests, peatlands and wet eucalypt forests while tracking threats including frequent fire, weeds, erosion and predation. Recovery is not a single green shoot. It is the return of relationships—and some communities need active help.

The hopeful truth is not that nature always bounces back. It is that careful observation can show us where resilience remains—and where care must enter the story. Recovery must be monitored, not merely imagined
04 — Remembering how to burn

Good fire begins with relationship.

For millennia, Indigenous peoples have used fire to care for Country: burning with close knowledge of season, moisture, wind, plants, animals and cultural responsibility. These practices are not a simple technique that can be detached from the people and places that hold the knowledge. They are systems of relationship.

Colonial fire exclusion interrupted many of these practices. In some fire-dependent landscapes, the absence of regular, attentive burning contributed to dense fuels and changed habitat. Today, Indigenous fire stewards, scientists and land managers are rebuilding partnerships around cultural burning and carefully planned fire.

Prescribed burns can reduce some fuels, support particular species and build a patchwork of vegetation ages. But they are not universal prescriptions. They require expertise, appropriate weather, clear objectives and deep local knowledge. Fire is powerful precisely because it is not simple.

Then the flame front passes.
The forest is quiet—but not empty.

05 — The first green

Life returns in verbs.

Rain darkens the ash. Fungal threads extend through altered soil. Insects enter. A red epicormic bud swells beneath black bark and breaks into green. Seeds respond to heat, smoke or the sudden opening of space. Animals return unevenly, following food and shelter back into the burn.

⌁ Days

Ash settles. Surviving roots and microorganisms begin responding to changed moisture and nutrients.

˱ Weeks

Resprouts appear. Germination begins. Pollinators and mobile animals revisit recovering patches.

♧ Years

Structure returns unevenly. Shrubs, grasses and young trees compete, shelter and reshape habitat.

✣ Decades

Old trees, hollows and complex relationships may return—if the next fires allow enough time.

The new forest is not a rewind. It may contain different proportions of species and new gaps in its memory. Ecology does not promise restoration to one frozen original state. It describes persistence through change—and gives us evidence for protecting what remains possible.

06 — Survival with help

Hope is a form of attention.

Post-fire recovery can be supported by protecting unburnt refuges, controlling invasive plants and predators, reducing erosion, reconnecting fragmented habitat and allowing enough time between fires. Remote sensing can reveal landscape-scale change; field surveys can show which species are returning; citizen observations can add thousands of small pieces to the record.

Fire ecologists increasingly study the whole moving system: how intensity, frequency, season and patchiness interact with topography, climate and the traits of plants and animals. The goal is not to remove fire from every landscape. It is to restore better rhythms while preventing avoidable harm.

There is humility in this work. We cannot command a forest back into being. We can reduce pressure, protect its surviving knowledge and learn when intervention helps. Sometimes stewardship means lighting a careful flame. Sometimes it means keeping the next one away.

Return to Surya Lok — Being

What survives is not what remains unchanged.

In Surya Lok, the truth of survival belongs to being: the reality that persists beneath our interpretations. The forest does not deny the fire. It carries the wound, alters its shape and begins again—not because destruction was harmless, but because life had stored more possibilities than the flames could see.

Perhaps endurance is not hardness. Perhaps it is the ability to keep a future hidden in the roots.
