Page II · The creature behind the headline

Meet the accused.

Before we place immortality on trial, our suspect deserves a proper introduction. This is not a germ, a myth or a miniature bear. It is a complete animal belonging to the phylum Tardigrada.

Look closely at a wet cushion of moss and you may discover an entire wilderness between its leaves. There, moving through a thin film of water, is an animal with a plump body, four pairs of legs and tiny claws. It walks with such an unhurried, slightly clumsy gait that the name Tardigrada means “slow stepper.” It is also called a water bear or moss piglet. 1

Most tardigrades are far too small to notice without magnification. They inhabit marine water, freshwater and damp terrestrial environments, including mosses and lichens. They are not lurking inside humans, and they are not dangerous to us.

Many feed by piercing plant, algal or fungal cells and drawing out their contents. Some species are predators that consume even smaller creatures.

The first surprise is not that it survives. It is that something so small is an animal at all.

An active tardigrade is not permanently living in a superhero state. It moves, feeds, grows and reproduces when its surroundings are suitable. It needs liquid water around it to remain active.

When that water disappears, however, certain tardigrades perform the biological manoeuvre that created their astonishing reputation.

One distinction will matter throughout this investigation: a tardigrade may survive a condition in which it cannot normally live, eat or reproduce. Survival is not comfort. Endurance is not immortality. The headline has already begun to wobble.

Page III · Exhibit A

The great disappearing act.

The moss dries. The watery paths between its leaves disappear. For us, the story would be ending. For the tardigrade, an extraordinary intermission is beginning.

State 01 · Active

Hydrated, moving, feeding and carrying out ordinary metabolism.

→

State 02 · Tun

Contracted, dehydrated and metabolically arrested until conditions improve.

Cryptobiosis is not resurrection. It is a reversible state of “hidden life”—an emergency pause so deep that the animal can appear almost lifeless.

As dehydration begins, the tardigrade retracts its legs and contracts into a compact form known as a tun. During anhydrobiosis— cryptobiosis triggered by drying— its water content falls dramatically and normal metabolic activity becomes extremely low or undetectable. 3

The animal is no longer strolling through moss, searching for food or carrying out an ordinary day of microscopic life. It is waiting.

Drying is dangerous because water helps biological molecules maintain their shape and function. Remove it carelessly and membranes, proteins and DNA may become damaged.

Tardigrades use protective strategies that vary between species. Researchers are especially interested in tardigrade-specific proteins that help vulnerable cellular material endure dehydration and other stresses. 4

It does not defeat death. It suspends ordinary life until the world becomes welcoming again.

Then comes the scene that earned the front-page headline. Water returns. The tun rehydrates. Its body expands. Its legs emerge. Movement begins again.

Researchers at the University of Queensland describe observing tardigrades emerge from dry moss after water was added. Under a microscope, the contracted form begins to unfold and the animal starts moving again. 4

Yet even this ability has conditions and limits. Species differ. The animal’s physiological state matters. The intensity and duration of the exposure matter.

“A tardigrade survived this experiment” cannot honestly be transformed into “every tardigrade survives everything.” That is precisely how remarkable evidence becomes a misleading legend.

Page IV · The prosecution presents four chambers

How harsh is harsh?

The courtroom doors open. Each chamber tests a different form of environmental stress. Imagine entering them as an active human—and remember that the tardigrade’s best-known feats often involve a protected, inactive state.

Chamber 01

Drought

The water around you drains away. Your cells begin losing the liquid in which their chemistry operates. A capable tardigrade contracts into a tun and enters anhydrobiosis. When water returns, it may resume activity.

Strategy: shut down and wait
Chamber 02

Freezing

Temperature falls and ice threatens delicate biological structures. Some tardigrades tolerate freezing by entering a cryptobiotic state, but tolerance varies between species and circumstances.

Evidence: species dependent
Chamber 03

Radiation

High-energy radiation can damage DNA and other cellular machinery. Tardigrade research points to several layers of defence—including protection and repair—not an invisible shield of invincibility.

Defence: protect and repair
Chamber 04

Vacuum

There is no breathable atmosphere and almost no pressure. Dehydrated tardigrades have survived controlled exposure during low-Earth-orbit research. That does not mean they naturally live, feed or reproduce in open space. 1

Breaking: survival is not thriving

Page V · Corrections and clarifications

Cross-examining the legend.

Select each sensational claim. The Myth Desk has obtained evidence the front page neglected to mention.

Verdict: false. Tardigrades can be injured and killed. Their active lifespans are finite, and even cryptobiosis has limits. Their fame comes from exceptional survival—not an escape from death.
Verdict: misleading. Short-term survival during an experiment is different from living actively in an environment. Temperature, duration, hydration, species and life stage can all affect the outcome.
Verdict: false. A tun is a living organism in cryptobiosis, with ordinary activity dramatically reduced. Rehydration restores activity. It does not resurrect an animal that was biologically dead.
Verdict: false. Tardigrada is an entire phylum containing many species. Their habitats, biology and environmental tolerances differ. A finding about one species cannot automatically be applied to every tardigrade.
Verdict: false. Many tardigrades inhabit ordinary aquatic or moist environments. Cryptobiosis is an emergency response to hostile conditions, not their preferred daily routine.

The defence calls three witnesses

Who protects a water bear?

No single magical ingredient explains every tardigrade or every form of stress. The defence presents a coordinated biological response.

Water

“Do not let the headline erase me. Active tardigrades still depend on liquid water. Their most famous trick begins when I disappear—and ends when I return.”

Protective proteins

“During dehydration, certain tardigrade-specific proteins can help stabilise vulnerable cellular material. Researchers study us because biological preservation may have uses far beyond the moss.” 4

Repair systems

“Survival is not only about preventing every injury. Cells may also need to recognise and repair damage after danger passes. Protection and recovery work together.”

Science becomes more wonderful— not less—when we replace “magic” with the patient question: how?

Page VI · Late space edition

A passenger beyond Earth.

The legend escaped the moss when tardigrades entered space research.

In a landmark low-Earth-orbit experiment, dehydrated tardigrades were exposed to the vacuum of space and different levels of solar radiation. Some survived after returning to Earth.

The result was astonishing, but carefully bounded: vacuum survival was strong, while ultraviolet radiation reduced survival. Outcomes differed between experimental treatments.

Read that again: some tardigrades survived an exposure experiment. They did not build a civilisation on a satellite.

Survival, recovery and successful reproduction are separate scientific questions.

Why send them at all? Their biology helps researchers investigate how animal cells respond to dehydration, radiation and microgravity.

It also sharpens an important question for astrobiology: if Earth organisms can endure more than expected, how carefully must we protect other worlds from biological contamination?

The tiny creature has therefore become more than a curiosity. It is a model for studying the boundaries of animal life— and for imagining how biological material could be protected during storage, medicine or long journeys. 4
